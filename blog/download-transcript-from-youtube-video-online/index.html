How to Download a Transcript From a YouTube Video

Published October 12, 2026 · 8 min read

People searching for a transcript and people searching for captions want the same bytes — but they discover the problem from different directions. The transcript searcher finds YouTube’s “Show transcript” panel, sees all the text sitting there, looks for a download button that isn’t there, and starts highlighting and dragging. This page is for that person: what that panel actually is, why it can’t export, how to get the transcript out in one step instead of forty, and what to do when a video genuinely has no text attached to it.

Transcript, caption, subtitle: same data, three jobs

A YouTube video carries one or more caption tracks — a list of cues, each with text and a start/end time, one set per language. From that single structure the platform produces everything you see: the overlay when you press CC (captions, the accessibility surface), the searchable block of text under the description (transcript, the reading surface), and the translated variants in other languages (subtitles, the cross-language surface). YouTube’s caption documentation describes the tiers — manual, automatic, translated — and it’s worth knowing which one you’re about to download, because it’s the difference between publishable text and notes-only text.

The consequence that matters here: anything that downloads captions downloads transcripts. There is no separate transcript API, no separate export, no special file. Search results that promise a “transcript downloader” as a different product from a “caption downloader” are describing one tool twice.

Why YouTube’s transcript panel has no download button

The panel is a reading and navigation aid, built so viewers can jump to the part they want and search inside a long talk — a feature for the platform, not an export pipeline for you. YouTube has never added a download control to it. So the copy-paste workaround is the only built-in option, and it has four costs people discover halfway through:

  • It’s a scroll-captured region, not a document. On long videos you select, scroll, select again, and hope you didn’t miss a chunk between grabs.
  • Timestamps come with it. Every line is prefixed with a start time you may or may not want; there is a toggle in the panel’s own menu, but hiding them doesn’t change what you get when you copy in every browser.
  • One language at a time. Translated tracks aren’t in the panel; they’re in the CC settings menu, and reaching them is several clicks per video.
  • Auto tracks paste as a wall. Repeated rolling cues, no punctuation, no speaker labels — the exact artefacts we catalogued in the clean-up guide.

We documented the full manual process, including what the pasted text needs before it’s usable, in how to copy all captions from a YouTube video. Read it if you want to stay inside YouTube; read the next section if you want a file.

Getting the transcript in one step

The one-step route is a caption tool that can output plain text. With YouTube Caption Downloader: paste the video URL, take the track list it returns, choose the language and kind, pick TXT, download. That’s the transcript of the whole video in a text file, including the parts the panel made you scroll for. No signup, no install, no limit — the terms the site states on its own homepage — and it works on Shorts and YouTube Music links as well as regular videos. The scored assessment is our tool review; the format decision is covered in the download walkthrough.

Two choices inside that step do most of the quality work:

  1. Pick the manual track if it’s listed. Creator-uploaded captions arrive punctuated and correctly spelled, so the TXT needs no editing. Automatic tracks are fine for personal research and miserable to clean.
  2. Pick the source language, not a translation, unless you’re reading for comprehension in another language. Translated transcripts inherit every error the source track made.

When you want the transcript with timestamps

Sometimes the times are the point: you need to know when something was said to cut a clip, cite a lecture, or find the moment again. Then download SRT instead of TXT and keep every cue’s start and end time. The same tool produces either from the same track, so the honest workflow for research is to grab both — search the clean TXT for the phrase, then look up its timecode in the SRT. Cue anatomy, the difference between SRT’s HH:MM:SS,mmm and VTT’s dot notation, and how to shift timings after re-cutting are all in our caption extractor with timestamps guide.

Your own videos: get it from Studio

If the video is yours, don’t use a third-party tool at all. YouTube Studio’s Subtitles tab lists your tracks per language and downloads the file you or your editor uploaded — the highest-fidelity copy that exists, with no re-encoding of someone else’s auto track. Google’s subtitle documentation covers that route and how to edit and re-upload. This is also the fix when the automatic transcript of your own video is wrong: download it, correct the names and punctuation, upload it back as the manual track, and it stops being a problem for everyone downstream.

When a video has no transcript at all

Occasionally the panel is empty and the extractor returns nothing: the creator turned captions off, or it’s a fresh upload YouTube hasn’t processed, or the audio is music with no spoken content. Nothing extracts text from a video that has none — no downloader, no extension, no script. Your only option is automatic speech recognition over the audio: a transcription service or a local model, which is a different (and usually paid, slower, less accurate on accents and jargon) job than this page’s. And the access cases people ask about — private, unlisted-with-restrictions or age-restricted videos — aren’t a tool limitation; no anonymous request can read them. Five things worth doing with the transcript

  • Read a 90-minute talk in six minutes. Skim the TXT, watch the ten minutes that matter. The single best use of transcripts for anyone who learns from video.
  • Draft the article the video should have had. A transcript is a first draft with the structure missing; the repurposing pipeline is in our extraction guide.
  • Fact-check and cite. Search exact wording instead of scrubbing a timeline, then quote it with its timestamp.
  • Feed a model something it can’t watch. Language models read transcripts; the video is inaccessible to them, the TXT is not. Clean it first — duplicated rolling cues quietly degrade summaries.
  • Audit your own channel’s content for keywords and claims, or hand the file to a translator instead of paying for a re-transcription.

That last step is where a clean file pays off: summarisers do far better work on de-duplicated text than on a raw caption dump. And the etiquette line is worth restating: reading someone’s transcript is ordinary; publishing it as your own article is their words with your name on them.

Frequently asked questions

Is a YouTube transcript the same thing as captions?

In practice yes: the transcript panel you see under a video is drawn from the same caption track the CC button uses. The word transcript describes the reading use, caption describes the on-screen use — the underlying data is identical, so anything that downloads captions gives you the transcript.

Why is there no download button on YouTube’s transcript?

Because YouTube built that panel as an accessibility and search feature, not an export tool. It shows the text, lets you jump to timestamps and search within the video, but the only way to move it out is selecting it and copying. A caption downloader exists precisely because there is no export.

Can I download a transcript for a video with no captions?

Not as a download. There is no text to extract, so you would need speech-to-text transcription of the audio instead, which is slower, less accurate on accents and jargon, and usually metered or paid. Automatic captioning covers most uploads, so check the CC button before assuming a video has nothing. For everything that does have a track, this tool exports it as text in one step.

Can I get a transcript with timestamps for the whole video?

Yes, by downloading the track as SRT or VTT rather than TXT. Both keep every cue’s start and end time, which is what you want for clip hunting and editor work; our timestamp guide covers the cue format and how to shift timings after a re-cut.
