Download YouTube Captions Without Signing In to Google

Published October 12, 2026 · 8 min read

A surprising number of “free” caption downloaders ask you to sign in with Google before handing over a text file. They don’t have to. Caption tracks on public videos are readable by anyone who can open the page — including a browser window with no cookies and no account. This is the account-free version of the job: why the login walls exist, what each method actually exposes about you, the three cases where a sign-in is genuinely required, and the red flags that should make you close the tab.

Why no account is needed for a public caption file

YouTube stores captions as separate tracks attached to a video, and a public video’s page is public — the same reason you can watch it logged out. Reading those tracks is an ordinary unauthenticated request. Google’s own captions documentation makes the asymmetry visible: listing tracks is comparatively open, while downloading through the official API demands OAuth. That OAuth step is what most third-party tools route around by using the same public paths the player itself uses. The practical conclusion: no Google account is required to get captions from a public video, and a site that says otherwise is describing its business model, not a technical limit.

What a login wall is really doing

Once you accept that the login isn’t needed, the reasons for the wall are obvious:

  • Identity attachment. Free tool, paid audience. Your email plus the categories of videos you process is a list someone can monetise, and it’s why “free with no limits” pages still want an account.
  • Quota disguise. Some services do have real rate limits, and an account is how they ration them. Legitimate — but it means “free” comes with a ceiling you should ask about before committing.
  • Credential harvesting. The worst case is a page that mimics Google’s sign-in screen. It exists in every download-tools niche because the traffic is high-intent and the visitors are in a hurry.

Our rule is simple: if a caption download sits behind a login, we leave. That leaves exactly two categories of account-free route — a browser tool that says so up front, and something running on your own machine.

The no-signin workflow we use

YouTube Caption Downloader is the one we keep bookmarked, and its homepage is explicit: no sign-up, no email, no credit card, no download limit, nothing to install. Paste the video URL, take the track list it returns — manual, auto-generated and translated — and download SRT, VTT or TXT. We tested it in a fresh private window with cookies cleared and got the same result as a normal tab: a file, no prompt, no account. Two habits reduce what you hand over without changing the workflow:

  • Strip the tracking parameters. A link copied from a newsletter or a social post often carries a campaign tag. Paste the bare watch?v=ID or youtu.be/ID form — the ten-character video ID is all the tool needs, and it also sidesteps playlist parameters that break some extractors.
  • Use a private window when you’re working on a borrowed or shared machine, so nothing about the session outlives the tab.

If your threat model is “no third party may learn which video I pulled”, the account-free answer is local: yt-dlp with --skip-download --write-subs never involves anyone but YouTube.

The three cases that do need a sign-in

  1. Private or unlisted-to-you videos. The track exists but the page doesn’t, so the request has to prove entitlement. No anonymous tool can help, and one claiming to is lying or scraping with stolen credentials.
  2. Age-restricted videos. YouTube gates these behind an authenticated, eligible account.
  3. Your own channel. For your uploads, skip third parties entirely: YouTube Studio’s Subtitles tab downloads the file you uploaded, and Google’s subtitle documentation covers that route. It’s the highest-fidelity copy available because it’s the source.

Everything else — public video, captions exist, you want the text — works logged out.

Red flags: close the tab

  • A Google sign-in screen on a site that isn’s google.com. This is credential phishing, full stop.
  • “Verify to unlock your download” for a text file — surveys, app installs, “complete one offer”. There is no verification step in a legitimate caption download.
  • A .exe or .dmg appearing instead of a .srt, or a “downloader manager” required to “speed up” the process.
  • Countdown timers and server queues for ten lines of text. Artificial scarcity is the tell.
  • Terms you can’t find. A tool that won’t say what it stores is a tool that stores things.

On the other side of the line: YouTube’s terms of service and a tool’s own disclosure still apply to you. Account-free means anonymous, not licence-free — the transcript belongs to whoever created it, and republishing someone’s captions as your own article is a copyright problem however politely you obtained the file.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a Google account to download YouTube captions?

No, not for a public video that already has captions. The caption track is attached to a page anyone can open, so a browser tool can read it without knowing who you are. YouTube Caption Downloader states no signup and no email on its homepage, and we have never been asked for one.

Why do some caption downloaders force me to sign in?

Because the login is the product. A Google sign-in lets a site tie your downloads to an identity, sell that list, or harvest the account through a lookalike page. Nothing about reading a caption track requires it, so treat a login wall as a reason to leave rather than an obstacle to get past.

When is signing in actually necessary?

Three cases: the video is private or unlisted to you, it is age-restricted and YouTube wants to confirm eligibility, or you are downloading captions for your own channel through YouTube Studio or the Data API. Outside those, an anonymous request is enough.

Does an account-free caption tool see my other browsing?

No, in the ordinary case. It sees the URL you paste, the fact that a request came from your browser, and whatever its analytics collect. That is much narrower than a browser extension, which can read every page it has host permission for while installed — the trade-off we cover in the no-extension guide.
