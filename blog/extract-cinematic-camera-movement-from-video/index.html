Extract Cinematic Camera Movement From a Video, Precisely

Published October 13, 2026 · 8 min read

“Dolly in” is a correct description of a hundred different shots, and only one of them is the one you are trying to recreate. Cinematic camera language is layered: the move, its speed curve, its height, the lens rendering it, and the reason it exists in the story. Extracting at that level is what separates a generated clip that feels filmed from one that feels simulated.

Why naming the move is not enough

Compare two shots both describable as a slow dolly-in: one at knee height on an 18mm lens with visible parallax and a hard ground plane, another at chest height on an 85mm with compression and no foreground. Same verb; completely different feeling. The difference lives in five properties that most prompts never mention — and that a well-extracted description can surface if you know what to ask for. This is the layer above the basic movement vocabulary.

The five cinematographic properties

PropertyWhat to stateWhy it changes the shot
Heightankle / knee / chest / eye / overheadDetermines power dynamics and what occludes what
Lens feelwide distortion, normal, long-lens compressionControls background scale and parallax intensity
Speed curveconstant, easing in, easing out, acceleratingHuman-camera moves ease; robots do not, and viewers notice
Motivationfollowing a subject, revealing, withholdingTells the generator where the frame should end up
Physicalitylocked-off, tripod fluid-head, gimbal, handheld, Steadicam driftThe micro-motion signature that reads as a human operating a camera

The underlying grammar of real camera work — tracking shots, dollies, cranes — is summarised well in the general cinematography literature; what matters here is that these five slots are writable in a prompt.

How extraction reads them

A visual analysis pass can genuinely see some of this and infer the rest. Parallax magnitude gives it lens feel and distance; horizon micro-motion gives it the physicality of the rig; subject scale change gives it speed. What it usually will not report unprompted is height as a number and motivation, because both require knowing what the shot is for.

So run the reference through SocialToPrompt and take its camera field as the start of a sentence rather than the whole of one: the tool describes the move, the framing, the light and the pacing — and you add height and intent. The verification pass

Four checks, each under ten seconds, that upgrade an extraction from plausible to accurate:

  1. Corners for rotation; background scale for translation versus zoom.
  2. Foreground occlusion. If something passes in front of the lens, there is a real dolly path and a wide lens — say so.
  3. Easing. Watch the start and stop. Most mechanical-feeling renders begin at full speed; most real shots ease in.
  4. Height guess. Where the horizon sits relative to the subject tells you whether the camera was at chest height or on the floor. Write it in inches or centimetres if you can; “camera at 40cm” is obeyed surprisingly often.

Every check you perform is a term you now own, which is the quiet compounding benefit of this workflow — see the three-pass reverse-engineering method.

Translating into a generated shot

Assemble in the order your target model weights, and this is the shape that survives across families:

A woman crosses an empty car park at dusk. Camera at hip height, 35mm feel, slow lateral track
right-to-left with easing at both ends, slight gimbal drift; sodium lamps behind her flare past
the frame edge; the background compresses rather than sliding. Teal shadows, warm highlights,
moderate contrast, fine grain. Eight seconds, ending with her leaving frame right.

Note the three things a naive prompt would omit: the easing, the compression-versus-slide distinction, and the flare passing the frame edge — all physical statements, all cheap in words and expensive in feel. For the equivalent in tag form for camera-literal models, see Hailuo’s camera tags; for multi-shot work, extracting shot lists.

The one property extraction will never hand you is why. A move that exists because a character is discovering something needs the same sentence in the prompt: “the camera finds the door as she notices it”. That is a creative decision, and it is yours.

Frequently asked questions

What makes camera language cinematic rather than generic?

Five properties beyond the verb: camera height, lens feel, the speed curve including easing, what motivates the move, and the physicality of the rig — tripod, gimbal, handheld or locked-off. Naming the movement alone describes a hundred different shots.

Can AI extract those properties from a reference clip?

Partly. Parallax, scale change and micro-motion give a model lens feel, speed and rig behaviour; height and motivation are usually missing because they require knowing what the shot is for. Treat the extracted camera line as the start of a sentence and add the other two yourself.

How do I describe easing in a prompt?

Say it in plain words: 'slow push in easing to a stop', or 'lateral track with easing at both ends'. Most renders feel mechanical because the camera begins at full speed, and a two-word easing clause fixes that more reliably than adjectives about smoothness.

Why does my generated shot lack depth even with a dolly prompt?

Almost always because the lens feel and foreground are unspecified. A dolly reads as depth only when parallax is visible, so add 'wide lens, foreground objects sliding past the frame edge' or 'long lens, background compressing' and the move acquires space.
