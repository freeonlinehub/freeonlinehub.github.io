Extract Video Transition Prompts With AI

Published October 13, 2026 · 7 min read

A transition is the one thing in a video that exists between two shots, which is precisely where frame-based analysis is weakest. Getting a usable prompt out of a whip pan or a match cut means understanding which transitions are shootable in a single generation and which are an edit trick you will have to rebuild yourself.

Why transitions are the hardest extraction

Temporal analysis works by comparing frames inside a window. A transition lasts a quarter to half a second, often contains motion blur, and by definition spans two different scenes — so an extractor will either miss it entirely or describe it as an incoherent third shot. Longer transitions (a light sweep over two seconds, a morph) survive; the snap cuts and whip pans that dominate short-form rarely do.

That asymmetry defines the workflow: extract the shots, then author the seam by hand.

The transition families that prompt well

TypeMechanismSingle-generation?
Match cutShape or motion matches across the cutNo — two generations plus an edit
Whip panFast horizontal rotation with blurYes, at the end of one shot
Light sweep / flare wipeBright element passes the lensYes
MorphContinuous transformation of one subject into anotherYes, if both states are described
Foreground occlusion wipeObject crosses the lens, frame goes darkYes
Speed rampSlow to fast within a shotPartly — describe as one motion
Jump cutRemoval of frames in the same setupNo — purely an edit decision

The naming conventions are standard cinematography vocabulary; see match cuts and the broader shot terminology.

What extraction can and cannot report

Give the extractor a window inside a shot rather than across a boundary and it behaves normally: SocialToPrompt reads subject, camera, lighting, pacing and style from a clip and returns them as structured text, with motion and camera analysed as explicit stages. What it will not reliably tell you is where a cut sits, what the two sides of a match cut had in common, or how fast a whip pan actually was.

So the practical loop is: run the reference through extraction for the shot language, then add the transition yourself as a clause at the end of one prompt or the start of the next.

Writing the seam between two generations

SHOT A (ends with the transition):
A barista slides a finished cup left across the counter; camera locked-off at bar height, warm
window key; 5 seconds, ENDS as the cup crosses the lens and fills the frame with dark ceramic.

SHOT B (starts from the same device):
Opens with the dark ceramic filling the entire frame, then pulling back to reveal a different
café, cool daylight, overhead framing; the cup is lifted away and the room appears.

The occlusion wipe works because both prompts describe the same visual event from opposite ends. That is the general rule for seams: name the shared visual state, and let the cut hide inside it. With the same logic, a whip pan becomes “ends panning hard right, motion blurred” plus “opens mid-pan settling left”, and a light sweep becomes “a bright element crosses the lens, frame blows out” plus “starts overexposed, exposure settles”.

When to build it in the edit

Two honest cases belong in an NLE rather than a prompt. Match cuts depend on precise geometric correspondence between frames — render both sides, then cut on the matching shape, and if you must, morph the last frame of A toward the first of B. Speed ramps are also an editing operation, since a single generation is unlikely to change pace cleanly.

Which means the prompt job is to produce two clips that can be joined: same lens feel, same light direction, matching framing height, and an end/start state designed for each other. That is the same constants discipline used in multi-shot sequences — see extracting shot lists and prompts and Seedance multi-shot prompting.

For reference-driven work, the transition is often the actual mechanism you were trying to steal: in that case, extract both shots separately, and keep the seam description in your .txt as the transferable idea — the method is in reverse engineering. If your problem is instead that a cut is missing from the extraction entirely, that is normal, and the diagnostic for why is inside the analysis pipeline.

Frequently asked questions

Why do extracted prompts miss transitions and cuts?

Because a transition lasts a fraction of a second and spans two different scenes, so it falls outside the temporal window motion analysis uses. Extractors describe the interior of shots well; boundaries are usually lost or reported as an incoherent third shot.

Which transitions can a single generation actually produce?

Whip pans, light sweeps and flare wipes, foreground occlusion wipes, and continuous morphs — anything where the change happens inside one shot. Match cuts, jump cuts and speed ramps are editing operations rather than generation operations.

How do I make two generated clips join seamlessly?

Design the shared visual state and describe it from both ends: shot A ends as the cup fills the frame with dark ceramic, shot B opens on dark ceramic filling the frame then pulling back. Match lens feel, light direction and camera height as well.

Can AI tell me where the cuts are in a video?

That's shot detection rather than prompt extraction, and it's a different tool — segment first, then describe each shot with a prompt extractor. Trying to extract one prompt for a whole edited sequence just averages its shots together.
