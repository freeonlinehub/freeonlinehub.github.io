Free YouTube Caption Downloader Online: 5 Ways That Still Work

Published October 12, 2026 · 9 min read

“Free YouTube caption downloader online” is one search phrase covering five completely different products: a browser tool, YouTube’s own transcript panel, a command-line program, a browser extension, and Google’s official API. They cost different amounts, leak different amounts of your data, and produce very different files. We tested all five on the same videos — a 47-minute podcast, a 9-minute tutorial with only auto-captions, a Shorts clip and a lecture with six translated tracks — so you can pick the right one for the job instead of whichever page ranked first.

Before you trust any page, check what “free” means

Four things get sold as “free caption downloader”, and only one of them is actually free:

  • Free with no account — paste a link, get a file. This is the only genuinely free version of the job.
  • Free with a login — the page wants a Google or email account. Captions on public videos do not require authentication to read, so a login wall is a data-collection decision, not a technical need.
  • Free with a trial — a desktop app or “online converter” that lets you download one or two files and then asks for a licence.
  • Free with an install — the download button quietly hands you an .exe, a .dmg or an extension prompt. This is the one that hurts.

There is also a fifth category — ad-farmed pages that show a fake “Your caption is ready” button over the real one. A good tell: a page that asks you to complete a survey, “verify you are not a robot” three times, or wait for a “server queue” to fetch ten lines of text. Caption tracks are small files; none of that is part of the job.

Method 1: a browser caption downloader (fastest for one video)

This is what most people want, and it’s the method we default to. YouTube Caption Downloader does exactly one thing: you paste a video URL, it lists every caption track that video exposes — manual captions uploaded by the creator, YouTube’s auto-generated track, and the translated tracks derived from either — and downloads whichever one you pick as SRT, VTT or plain TXT. The homepage states the terms plainly: no signup, free to use, no download limit, and nothing installed on your machine. Our full hands-on review of YouTube Caption Downloader covers the fine print and where it loses points.

In testing it handled all four videos, including the Shorts clip and the lecture with six languages, in a couple of seconds per fetch. It fails in the two places any tool of this kind fails: videos that are private or age-restricted, and videos with no caption track at all. That is a limit of the source, not the tool — see the troubleshooting section in our guide to downloading captions from a YouTube video for what to do when the list comes back empty.

Best for: one video at a time, any device with a browser, zero setup. Not for: batch jobs across a whole channel, or people who refuse to let a third-party site see which URL they pasted.

Method 2: YouTube’s own transcript panel (free, but copy-only)

Every video with captions has a Show transcript link under the description. Open it and you get a scrollable text panel with timestamps you can switch off from the panel’s own menu — and that is the entire feature set. YouTube gives you no download button here, which surprises a lot of people searching for exactly this.

So the workflow is manual: turn timestamps off, select the text in the panel, copy it, paste it into a document. On a 9-minute tutorial that is fine. On the 47-minute podcast it produced the mess we’ve seen many times — auto-caption segments repeat themselves, line breaks land mid-sentence, and on the translated tracks the panel only shows one language at a time. We wrote a dedicated walkthrough of the copy-everything approach and its failure modes if you want the details before trying it. One genuine advantage: it works on videos where you’re signed in and can see the player — nothing third-party involved.

Best for: grabbing a quote or two while watching. Not for: getting a usable file, timestamps, or anything longer than a few minutes.

Method 3: the command line (free, local, batch-capable)

yt-dlp, the open-source media archiver maintained on GitHub, pulls caption tracks without touching the video. The flags that matter:

yt-dlp --skip-download --write-subs --write-auto-subs \
       --sub-langs "en.*,es,ja" --convert-subs srt "VIDEO_URL"

--skip-download keeps it text-only, --write-subs grabs creator-uploaded tracks, --write-auto-subs adds YouTube’s automatic ones, --sub-langs filters by language, and --convert-subs normalises everything to one format. Point it at a playlist URL and you get a folder of files while you do something else — which is the one thing no browser tool can match.

The cost is honesty about who it suits: you need Python installed, a terminal, and a willingness to re-run it when YouTube changes something and the tool needs updating. It also writes files with long auto-caption runs duplicated across segments, which you then clean by hand. For a recurring research pipeline it’s the right tool; for a single transcript before a meeting, it’s ten times more work than Method 1.

Best for: batches, playlists, channels, automation, people who want nothing sent to a third-party site. Not for: anyone who dislikes the terminal.

Method 4: browser extensions

Extensions add a subtitle button to the YouTube player itself, which is convenient in the same way that letting a stranger keep a spare key is convenient. The permission model is the problem: to work on YouTube the extension needs to read and change data on youtube.com — and in practice most of these request access to all sites, which is what Chrome warns you about on the install screen. Extensions also update silently after review, and the caption-download niche has had more than one case of a popular tool being sold and quietly turning into an ad injector.

The deeper issue is that this job doesn’t need an extension. A download happens once, on a page you chose, and takes four steps. Installing persistent access to your browsing to save four steps is a bad trade, which is why we keep this method near the bottom of the list. If you absolutely want a button inside the player, read the extension’s permission list first and prefer one that only touches youtube.com — the host_permissions key in its manifest is what decides how much of your browsing it can read.

Best for: daily heavy users who have audited the extension. Not for: everyone else, which is nearly everyone.

Method 5: the YouTube Data API

The official route exists and is nearly always the wrong one for this task. The Data API’s captions resource can list and download tracks, but download requires a Google account’s OAuth consent, works only with tracks the authenticated party is entitled to, and carries a quota weight high enough that a handful of downloads eats a meaningful slice of the default daily allowance. It is the right answer for one audience: developers building a product on their own channel’s captions inside a larger pipeline. For a person who wants one transcript, it is a project, not a tool.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best free YouTube caption downloader online?

For one video at a time in a browser, a dedicated web tool is the fastest option. We use YouTube Caption Downloader: paste the URL, pick manual, auto-generated or translated captions, download SRT, VTT or TXT with no account. If you need batches or full control of the file, use yt-dlp instead.

Is it legal to download YouTube captions?

Downloading a caption track for research, note-taking or accessibility is a normal everyday use, but the text itself usually belongs to the creator. Publishing someone else’s transcript verbatim, or repackaging it as your own content, is where you run into copyright problems. See YouTube’s terms of service and the US Copyright Office’s fair-use guidance before redistributing.

Why do some free caption downloaders ask me to log in?

They don’t need to. Caption tracks on public videos are reachable without a Google account, so a login prompt is a data-collection choice, not a technical requirement. Any page that gates a caption download behind Google sign-in should be closed. Our no-sign-in guide lists the red flags.

Can I get captions from a video that has none?

No tool can extract a track that does not exist. If a creator disabled captions and YouTube has not generated an automatic track, the only way to get text is to transcribe the audio yourself with a speech-to-text service, which is a different job from downloading captions.
