Kling AI Prompt Extractor From a Video, Field by Field

Published October 13, 2026 · 7 min read

Kling — Kuaishou’s video model, and one of the most-used generators for short-form work — has a particular appetite: it renders explicit motion well and punishes ambiguity about who or what is moving. An extractor gets you most of the way; the last 20% is knowing which fields to keep, which to compress and which to write yourself. Here is the field-by-field version.

What Kling rewards in a prompt

  • Motion specificity. Not “she walks” but “she walks toward camera, coat swinging, three steps”. Motion is where this family shines.
  • One dominant action. Competing actions produce warping, especially around hands and hair.
  • Physical camera language. Named moves and heights; vague adjectives about “cinematic feel” do nothing.
  • Material and light behaviour. Wet surfaces, glass, fabric and skin are rendered convincingly when described as surfaces.
  • Short, front-loaded clauses. Subject, then motion, then camera, then light — not a paragraph.

Assembling a Kling prompt

[subject], [one action with direction and magnitude], [camera: one verb + height + stability],
[light: source + direction + quality], [surface/material cues], [palette + contrast],
[duration], [end state]

Example:
A barista in a dark apron presses a portafilter, two-handed and firm; camera locked-off at
counter height with slight handheld drift; hard key from a window camera-left, deep shadow
right; steel and ceramic surfaces with specular highlights; muted warm grade, moderate contrast;
5 seconds; ends on the tamper lifted, steam beginning.

Four clauses that Kling particularly rewards: the two-handed action detail (hands are a known failure point), camera height, specular surface behaviour, and the end state.

Camera and motion verb list

Use one camera verb and one or two subject-motion verbs per prompt. The set that behaves predictably: push in / pull back, track left / right, pan, tilt, crane up, orbit, follow behind, locked-off, handheld drift. For the physics behind them — why a dolly and a zoom render differently — see extracting camera movement with AI, and dolly moves for the underlying technique.

Motion modifiers that scale reliably: slow, steady, abrupt, gentle, accelerating. Avoid frame counts and timecodes; describe timing as behaviour instead (“as the door opens”).

Common Kling prompt failures

  • Warping hands and faces → too many simultaneous actions; cut one.
  • Background swimming → a camera verb without a height or stability statement.
  • Flat, generic look → inherited mood words from the extractor; replace with palette and light direction.
  • Subject morphs mid-shot → the noun changed between clauses; repeat it verbatim.
  • Nothing happens → the prompt described a scene, not an event. Every Kling prompt needs a verb the render can execute.

Frequently asked questions

Can I extract a prompt from a video that works in Kling?

Yes, with a short rewrite. Extraction returns subject, motion, camera, lighting, pacing and style fields; Kling wants them compressed into front-loaded clauses with one dominant action, an explicit camera verb and a stated end state — the last of which no extractor supplies.

What camera words does Kling respond to?

Plain movement verbs with height and stability: push in, pull back, track left or right, pan, tilt, crane up, orbit, follow behind, locked-off, handheld drift. One per prompt. Timing words like slow, abrupt or accelerating work better than frame counts.

Why do hands and faces distort in my Kling renders?

Almost always because the prompt asked for more than one simultaneous action. Kling handles a single dominant motion cleanly; competing verbs produce warping around hands, hair and jaws. Cut an action before you tweak the wording.

What does an extractor not tell me for Kling?

Duration and end state, light direction, and how much of the shot is motion rather than composition. Those four are the difference between a Kling prompt that renders and one that merely describes.
