Real pricing after failure rates

Every platform publishes credit costs. None publishes failure rates. On Higgsfield, a 15-second 720p Seedance 2.0 video eats roughly 90 credits; a $34 / month Plus plan gives about 700 of them, which lands at 11 to 12 videos per month before waste. The hidden term is that 20 to 30 percent of generations produce unusable output while you are dialing in prompts — and failed generations still burn credits.

Real per-usable-10-second-clip math across the platforms, after the waste factor:

  • Dreamina: $0.15 to $0.20 per usable clip.
  • Higgsfield: $0.45 to $0.55 per usable clip.
  • Runway: $2.50 to $3.00 per usable clip.
  • fal.ai: $0.30 to $0.50 per-second at API rate, varies by implementation.

The single largest cost-saver: draft with Fast mode (where available) at half the credits, then only render your winners with Standard mode. Most platforms expose the toggle; use it. As a smaller second rule — on Higgsfield specifically, credits expire after 90 days, so top-up packs vanish if you forget about them. Budget for what you actually use this month.

Setup on Dreamina in 10 minutes

For a first test, Dreamina's the fastest path because it costs about a tenth of a Runway generation per attempt.

  1. Open dreamina.com in any browser.
  2. Sign up or log in with a Google account.
  3. Hit AI Video on the left sidebar.
  4. Choose Seedance 2.0 from the model selector. Depending on the region it may be labeled "Advanced" or "Pro."
  5. Paste a quick test prompt: A white cat leaps onto a wooden kitchen counter, morning window light casting long shadows, medium shot, natural film grain.
  6. Set aspect ratio to 16:9, duration to 6 seconds.
  7. Hit Generate and wait 30–90 seconds for the output.

Diagnostic pass: if the model delivers a cat jumping onto a counter under soft morning light, the workflow is working end-to-end. If Seedance 2.0 does not appear in your model selector, the platform has not shipped it in your region yet — switch to Higgsfield for the same model.

The face-filter workaround that works

The single most common complaint across every Seedance community right now is the face filter. It blocks AI-generated characters, stylized illustrations, side profiles, and even cartoon faces with sunglasses. The detection runs as computer vision on your reference images before the prompt is processed, which is why rewriting the prompt changes nothing: the filter has already failed the request before the model ever sees the words.

What actually passes, ranked by reliability:

Grid overlay method (most reliable). Put solid grid lines over your reference image at 100 percent opacity. A 6×6 grid is the sweet spot for most images. The grid breaks up the pixel patterns face detection relies on, while Seedance still reads the character underneath. Tradeoff: faint grid artifacts can show through in the output. For social content and drafts it is worth it. For final renders, plan a clean-up pass.

AI-generated portrait swap. Instead of uploading a real photo, generate the portrait of your character using an image model and use that as the reference. The filter applies differently to illustrated and AI-generated faces. This is the cleanest output method but adds a step.

Composition tricks. Full-body shots where the face is a small portion of the frame, dynamic poses with hair or objects partially covering the face, and complex backgrounds all help. The rule of thumb is to make the image look less like an ID photo.

Methods that look promising but fail: semi-transparent grids (the detector sees through them). Cropping to hide part of the face (passes sometimes, kills character consistency between generations). Editing the prompt to mention generic characters (the prompt is not the problem).

The physics-first prompting framework

This is the framework that separates first-try chaos from usable output.

[subject] + [action] + [environment] + [camera] + [style] + [constraints]

Five fields. Each one has a job. None of them are optional.

Step 1: Name the shot. Start every prompt with the camera position. ECU (extreme close-up), OTS (over the shoulder), POV, wide, tracking — these are the first things the model processes. If the camera position is not stated, the model picks one and you lose a generation learning what it picked.

Step 2: Describe the physics chain, not the action. Seedance reads physics. Cause, reaction, redirection, impact, continuation. Adjectives produce nothing useful. Instead of car crashes into wall, write front bumper contacts concrete barrier, hood crumples upward from impact point, glass fragments scatter forward and upward, momentum transfers through chassis causing rear wheels to lift. The model renders each physical interaction in sequence; that is what the physics engine actually does.

Step 3: One camera, one movement. Seedance handles one continuous camera movement per generation. The moment a prompt asks for a pan that becomes a zoom that becomes a tracking shot, the model tries to do all three and the output is visual chaos. Pick one. Describe where the camera starts, what it moves through, and where it ends. If you need a cut, that is a separate generation.

Step 4: Anchor with material behavior. Describe what surfaces and materials do during the action. Cloth drapes. Liquid splashes. Metal deforms. Smoke disperses. This is the part Seedance's physics engine actually excels at, and it is what makes the viral clips read as real.

Step 5: Negative prompts for cleanup. Keep them short and specific to the actual problem. No jitter, no warping, no identity drift. Or no flickering, no floating limbs. Two or three targeted terms beat a long list every time. The result reads more like a storyboard annotation than a creative brief; that is the point.

Product photo to video, step by step

The e-commerce use case is the highest-leverage one for Seedance: a single image plus a physics prompt produces a publishable clip in under ten minutes once the framework is dialed in.

  1. Prep the image. Product on a clean background, well lit, high resolution. For lifestyle shots, make sure the product is the clear focal point. Seedance accepts up to 9 reference images, 3 video clips and 3 audio clips in a single generation, but for product videos one clean image plus a text prompt is enough to start.
  2. Tag the reference. Upload the image and tag it: @Image1 is the product, a [name] on a [surface]. Seedance uses these tags to bind the prompt to the correct input.
  3. Write the physics prompt. Not show the product in a cool way but something concrete: ECU of @Image1 on a marble countertop, camera pushes in slowly from medium to tight, morning light catches the surface edge creating a highlight sweep left to right, slight condensation forms on the surface, ambient kitchen sounds.
  4. Set 720p for drafts. Save credits. 1080p doubles consumption on most platforms and the quality difference only matters on the final export.
  5. Generate, evaluate, iterate. The first generation is a test. Look at what the model did right, adjust the physics chain, regenerate. Most creators land on a usable clip by generation two or three once the framework is in muscle memory.
  6. For final renders, switch to Standard mode, bump to 1080p if the platform supports it, and add brand-specific details to the prompt.

The end-to-end process takes about ten minutes once you know the sequence. The first time, budget thirty.

The Claude + Seedance two-step pipeline

Writing physics-first prompts from scratch every time is slow. The community pipeline converges on a clean shortcut.

The setup: describe what you want in plain language to Claude — I need a product showcase of a glass perfume bottle on a marble surface, morning light, premium feel, 10 seconds. Claude converts that into a structured Seedance prompt with the physics chain, camera position, material behavior and negative prompts already formatted.

Two reasons this pipeline beats direct prompting for batch production. First, you describe intent in natural language instead of forcing yourself to think in physics terminology every time. Second, Claude catches things a human first-time prompter forgets — like specifying a single camera movement or anchoring with material behavior. The pipeline becomes especially powerful at scale: write ten plain-language descriptions of ten different products, run them through Claude, paste the outputs into Seedance. What used to take an afternoon becomes an hour.

This two-step approach is also the natural hand-off point if you have a reference video rather than a text brief. The output saves the half-hour of staring at a reference clip trying to name its beats — the same beats you would otherwise feed back into Claude.

For briefs that start from a pattern rather than a reference, VideoSPrompt is the library path: working Seedance-style prompts tagged by model, motion style and outcome. Combined with the Claude round-trip, it gives you a "reference video — Claude translation — Seedance render" path and a "pattern library — Claude adaptation — Seedance render" path in the same toolchain.

When Seedance is not the right tool

Seedance 2.0 is the best publicly available AI video model for physics coherence, character lock and multi-shot consistency right now. That does not make it the right choice for every job.

  • High-volume social at the lowest cost. Kling 3.0 still wins this: native 4K at 60fps, globally available API at roughly $0.075 per second, more mature ecosystem. If you are producing 50 clips a week for TikTok, Kling's cost structure wins.
  • Broadcast-quality output with native audio. Google's Veo 3.1 has the best audio-video synchronization of any publicly available model. Bundled into Google Vids at no additional cost for Workspace users, which makes it the right pick for narrated walkthroughs and corporate-style product demos.
  • Longer-than-15-second coherent shots. Veo 3.1 handles coherent 60-second shots in demos. Seedance caps at 15 seconds per generation, so anything longer requires manual extension and character re-anchoring.
  • Open-source local deployment. Watch HappyHorse-1.0 through 2026; it briefly topped the Artificial Analysis leaderboard in early April 2026 before being taken offline, and an open-weights release would change the landscape. No stable access exists today.

Frequently asked questions

Which platform should I run Seedance 2.0 on?

Four platforms carry Seedance 2.0 right now: Dreamina (cheapest, around $0.11 per clip but with the most aggressive face filter), Higgsfield (community favorite, 70% discount in April 2026 for new signups but unavailable in the US and Japan), Runway (most expensive at roughly $2.24 per generation but globally available), and fal.ai (API-based per-second pricing aimed at developers). Outside the US: Higgsfield. Inside the US: Runway. Pure cost optimization above everything else: Dreamina, with the filter caveat.

How do you bypass the Seedance face filter?

The most reliable workaround is a solid grid overlay on your reference image at 100 percent opacity (a 6×6 grid is the sweet spot). The grid breaks up pixel patterns face detection relies on while leaving Seedance able to read the character underneath; some community browser tools automate it. Other options that pass inconsistently: replace the real reference with an illustrated or AI-generated portrait, or shift the composition so the face is a smaller portion of a full-body shot. Rewriting the prompt does nothing — the filter blocks before the prompt is processed.

Why does Seedance produce chaos instead of cinema?

Because Seedance reads physics, not adjectives. Words like cinematic, epic and amazing produce nothing useful. To get speed, describe what the physics would look like — fabric pulling taut against wind, dust scattering behind a pivot, liquid sloshing from deceleration. The second cause of chaos is combining camera movements: Seedance handles one continuous movement per generation, so a prompt that asks for pan-plus-zoom-plus-tracking tries all three and produces nothing clean. The third is ignoring shot sequence: a single line like a man comes home from work is too vague. Break it into beats the model can render individually.

What does "physics-first prompt" actually look like?

A Seedance prompt is: subject +amp; action +amp; environment +amp; camera +amp; style +amp; constraints, where each component is described as a chain of physical interactions rather than a mood. Instead of car crashes into wall, write front bumper contacts concrete barrier, hood crumples upward from impact point, glass fragments scatter forward and upward, momentum transfers through chassis causing rear wheels to lift. The model renders each interaction sequentially. Material behavior — cloth drapes, liquid splashes, metal deforms, smoke disperses — is the part Seedance's physics engine actually excels at, and that is what separates usable output from chaos.

How long does a usable Seedance clip take from a product photo?

About ten minutes once the framework is dialed in. First time, budget thirty minutes for the setup plus a couple of bad generations. The image-tag-and-prompt path runs in roughly 10 minutes: prep the image on a clean background, upload and tag it as your product reference, write a physics-first prompt (subject, action, environment, camera, style, constraints), generate at 720p in Fast mode to save credits, evaluate, refine, render the winner at 1080p. Most creators land on a publishable clip by generation two or three.
