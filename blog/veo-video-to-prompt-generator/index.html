Veo Video to Prompt Generator: Writing for an Audio-Aware Model

Published October 13, 2026 · 8 min read

Veo’s family of models differs from most of the short-form tools in one structural way: sound is part of the generation, not something you bolt on afterwards. That changes what a good prompt looks like — you are describing an audiovisual event, and a prompt written for a silent model leaves half the render to chance. Here is how to build a Veo-shaped prompt from a video you already admire.

What makes Veo prompting different

Three practical consequences of an audio-aware model. Describe what you hear — ambience, single effects, and any dialogue you want spoken — because silence is a choice the model will only make if you make it. Bind action to sound: “the door slams as the camera pushes in” gives the render a synchronisation cue rather than two unrelated lists. Keep camera language plain, since the model is trained on production captions and rewards the same verb vocabulary used elsewhere: push in, track, pan, tilt, crane, orbit, handheld, locked-off (see camera movement extraction).

The model family sits under Google’s DeepMind umbrella and is reachable through its product surfaces; the prompting approach is what matters here rather than which endpoint you use.

The scene, camera, sound structure

SCENE   one or two sentences: subject, place, time of day, one physical detail.
CAMERA  one movement, one height, one framing statement.
LIGHT   source, direction, quality — always named, never implied.
SOUND   ambience + one or two specific events; dialogue in quotes if wanted.
MOVE    what changes during the shot, and how it ends.

Five clauses, in that order, is the shape that behaves. It is also almost exactly what a good extraction returns — minus the sound, which a visual model cannot read off your reference unless the clip has an accessible transcript.

Sound design lines that actually render

  • One ambience, two events. “Rain on a tin awning; a bicycle bell; footsteps approaching on wet stone” is plenty. A list of eight sounds collapses into mud.
  • Tie events to visible motion. “The lid clangs shut as her hand leaves frame” gives the model a temporal anchor.
  • Quote dialogue sparingly and precisely. One short line per shot; long scripts degrade both the speech and the pacing.
  • Say what is absent when it matters. “No music” is a legitimate instruction — otherwise trained-on advertising habits creep in.

Worked example and iteration

Reference: 7s clip, bakery at dawn, dough punched, flour dust rises in a light shaft.

SCENE   A baker's hands punch down dough on a floured wooden bench in an empty bakery at dawn.
CAMERA  Locked-off, camera at bench height, medium close-up, slight handheld drift.
LIGHT   Single hard shaft of low sun from frame right, deep shadow on the left wall, dust visible.
SOUND   Room tone; the dough thud on impact; a short exhale. No music.
MOVE    Flour kicks up into the light shaft and settles; hands leave frame; ends on the dough.

Iterate one clause at a time, and in this order: camera, then light, then sound, then the change clause. Sound is last because changing a movement changes what the sound should sync to. Frequently asked questions

How is a Veo prompt different from a prompt for other video models?

Sound is part of the generation. A Veo-shaped prompt names an ambience and one or two specific audio events, ties them to visible motion, and states whether dialogue or music should be present. Camera and lighting language is the same plain production vocabulary used elsewhere.

Can I generate a Veo prompt from an existing video?

For the visual clauses, yes: extract subject, camera, lighting, pacing and style from the clip, then write the sound clause yourself. A visual extractor cannot hear anything, so the audio half of a Veo prompt is always your own work.

How much sound should one prompt describe?

One ambience and up to two discrete events, each anchored to something visible. Long sound lists collapse into noise, and unanchored events rarely sync with the action you wanted.

What order should I iterate in?

Camera, then light, then the change clause, then sound last — because a different camera movement changes what the sound needs to hit. Change one clause per run so you know which edit caused the improvement.
