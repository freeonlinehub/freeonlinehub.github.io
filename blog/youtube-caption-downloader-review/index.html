What YouTube Caption Downloader does

YouTube Caption Downloader is a free browser tool whose entire job is to pull caption tracks off YouTube videos. You give it a video URL; it returns every track the video has — manual captions the creator uploaded, YouTube’s auto-generated captions, and the auto-translated tracks derived from them — and hands you each one as a downloadable file in the format you choose. The homepage spells out the terms plainly: no sign-up, no email, no credit card and no download limit. The site states it is not affiliated with YouTube and reminds users to respect creators’ rights when reusing captions.

This one is the clean middle path — one page, no install, no account, one job done well. How to grab captions in four steps

The whole flow is exactly what the homepage promises — four steps, and you can’t get lost:

  1. Paste the YouTube link into the input box — a regular youtube.com/watch?v=… URL, a Shorts link, or a youtu.be share link all work.
  2. Find available captions — the tool fetches every caption track the video exposes and lists them in a clean table.
  3. Select your format — pick SRT, VTT or TXT from the row’s action menu.
  4. Click Download — the file lands on your machine instantly.

That’s it. No pop-ups, no captcha gauntlet, no waiting for a queued job — the tool runs client-side in your browser, which is also why it can credibly promise “no account”. There’s nothing to undo or clean up afterward, because nothing about the video or the link is stored on the tool’s side.

Manual, auto-generated and translated tracks

The table of available captions splits into three tiers, and they’re worth understanding because each one has a different sweet spot:

  • Manual captions. The track the creator wrote and uploaded themselves — highest accuracy, the one you should default to whenever it exists.
  • Auto-generated captions. YouTube’s own ASR pass, labelled (Auto-generated) in the track list. Available on most videos, even ones where the creator never wrote captions.
  • Auto-translated tracks. YouTube’s auto-translation pipeline generates these from the source captions in dozens of languages — so a video with only English captions can still surface Spanish, Japanese, Arabic and more, machine-grade but useful.

The practical pattern is: manual first, auto-generated second, auto-translated for cross-language work. Manual tracks are how the creator wanted their words to read; auto-generated tracks are only as accurate as the audio they came from — heavy accents, overlapping speakers and music can degrade them — and auto-translations are machine-grade by definition. For research, comprehension and drafting they’re excellent; for anything you publish under your own name, prefer the manual track where one exists. YouTube’s own caption help doc walks through how the three tiers differ.

SRT vs VTT vs TXT: which format to pick

The tool’s three output formats aren’t interchangeable, and picking the right one saves a conversion later:

  • TXT — plain text with timecodes stripped. This is the transcript format: paste it into a doc, turn a webinar into a blog post, drop a lecture into your notes, or hand it to an AI assistant to summarize. If the goal is reading or repurposing the words, download YouTube captions as TXT. Our guide to extracting clean subtitle text covers what to do with it next.
  • SRT — the SubRip format that became the universal subtitle standard: numbered cues with start and end timecodes. Premiere Pro, Final Cut, DaVinci Resolve, VLC and most video editors and media players expect it, so SRT is the safe default when the file’s destination is a video. Our VideoEditorOnline review walks through a free browser editor that burns SRTs in without uploading your footage.
  • VTT — WebVTT, the W3C-standard caption format (full spec) used by the HTML5 <track> element and modern web players. Same cue structure as SRT with richer styling hooks; pick it when the captions are headed for a website rather than an editor.

All three download from the same row, so the practical answer is: match the format to the destination. If you guessed wrong, re-extract — it costs you ten seconds, not a re-render. Where it works — and the videos it can’t touch

The headline scope is any public YouTube video with captions, including Shorts and YouTube Music links. That covers the long tail of cases most people actually care about: tutorials, lectures, podcasts, news clips, product reviews, conference talks. Paste, fetch, download.

The honest limits are spelled out on the homepage:

  • Private or age-restricted videos — the tool can’t reach these, and neither can anything else that isn’t signed in as the rightful viewer.
  • Videos with no captions at all — if the creator disabled captions and YouTube hasn’t generated any, there is nothing to download. Trying a different language sometimes helps when auto-translation hasn’t fired yet.
  • YouTube only — unlike the broader multi-platform extractors we’ve reviewed, it doesn’t reach Bilibili, Vimeo, TikTok or Dailymotion. If you research across platforms, look at the FLOWNIB extractor for that — if you live inside YouTube, this narrower scope is exactly what you want.

The site itself includes a “Not affiliated with YouTube” note, which is the standard disclosure for a third-party caption tool and a good reminder that how you reuse the captions matters as much as whether you downloaded them.

Six real-world uses for a YouTube caption file

YouTube captions are one of the most versatile assets in a content workflow, and “download YouTube captions” queries span a lot of jobs. Six we keep coming back to:

  • Turn videos into blog posts. A transcript is the fastest first draft there is — paste the TXT export into an editor, tighten it, add structure. Search engines can finally read what the video said.
  • Caption your own edits. Pull SRT files and burn them in with a browser editor — our free video tools roundup links a few that add subtitles without uploading footage.
  • Accessibility. SRT or VTT captions make a video usable for deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers, and for the majority of social viewers watching with the sound off.
  • Language learning. Auto-translated tracks make crude but effective dual subtitles: the original language on the video, your language in the file.
  • Research and quoting. Cite exact wording from a talk or interview without scrubbing the timeline, and search a long video like a document.
  • Competitive keyword research. Extract the transcript of a competitor’s most-viewed video and you can see exactly which phrases and hooks they repeat — audience research on a platter.

Where it falls short

No write-up is complete without the fine print. Three limits worth knowing before you bookmark it:

  • YouTube only. The scope is intentional and clearly stated, but if you need Bilibili, Vimeo or TikTok captions in the same flow, the broader extractor is the better fit.
  • It extracts, it doesn’t transcribe. The tool pulls existing caption tracks; it doesn’t transcribe audio itself. A video with no captions and no auto-captions sends you away empty-handed — there is literally no track to fetch.
  • No editing built in. Timing off, or a typo inherited from the source? You’ll fix that in a text editor or an SRT-aware tool after download — the extractor hands you the file and steps aside.

None of these are surprises — they’re the boundary of the tool’s one job — but they define who should look elsewhere: anyone needing multi-platform coverage, live transcription, batch processing, or in-browser subtitle editing should budget for a dedicated tool alongside this one.

Frequently asked questions

Is YouTube Caption Downloader really free?

Yes — the homepage is explicit about it. There is no sign-up, no email, no credit card and no download limit. Paste a YouTube URL, pick a track, and the file is yours.

Do I need to install anything?

No. It runs entirely in the browser. The only requirement is JavaScript enabled, which every modern browser already has. There is no extension, no command-line tool and no desktop install.

Which caption formats can I download?

SRT (SubRip), VTT (WebVTT) and plain text (TXT). Every track — manual, auto-generated or auto-translated — can be exported in any of the three formats.

Does it work on every YouTube video?

It works on any public video with captions, including Shorts and YouTube Music links. Private, age-restricted or unlisted videos that have no caption data will return nothing to download.

Can it grab auto-generated or translated captions?

Yes. Auto-generated captions show up in the track list labelled (Auto-generated), and auto-translated tracks appear as well — so you can pull captions in languages the creator never published.
