Download YouTube Captions Without an Extension (No Install Needed)

Published October 12, 2026 · 8 min read

Somewhere in the search results for caption downloading, a browser extension started looking like the convenient option: a button appears in the YouTube player, you click, you get the file. It is convenient. It is also the only method that asks for permanent access to your browser to solve a problem that takes four steps once. Here are the three ways to download YouTube captions with nothing installed — plus the honest reasons you might still pick the extension anyway.

What a caption extension actually asks for

Read the install prompt instead of clicking past it. To add a button inside the YouTube player, an extension needs permission to read and change data on the pages it runs on — and the reference documentation for the host_permissions manifest key is clear about how wide that door is. Two things make the caption-download niche worse than it looks:

  • Scope creep in the manifest. A tool that only needs youtube.com often ships with <all_urls> so it can inject UI anywhere, phone home, or check ads. That is read-access to your banking tab, granted to a subtitle button.
  • Ownership changes after install. Extensions get sold. The reviewed code you approved can be replaced by a new owner’s build while the permissions stay exactly as they were, which is why the store started showing “this extension has changed” warnings at all.

Set against that: the actual job is fetching a small text file that already exists publicly. Persistent access is a strange payment for a one-off download, and it’s the reason no route below involves installing anything.

Route 1: a browser caption downloader (the one we use)

Open YouTube Caption Downloader in a tab, paste the video URL, and the page lists every caption track the video exposes — creator-uploaded manual captions, YouTube’s auto-generated track, and translated versions — each downloadable as SRT, VTT or TXT. No extension, no desktop app, no account. The site states its own terms directly: no signup, free to use, and it works on public videos including Shorts and YouTube Music links. Our full review scores it and lists what it doesn’t do.

What it gives you over the extension is containment. The access begins when you paste a URL and ends when you close the tab; there is nothing left running in your browser to update, get sold, or watch other sites. It’s also identical on a work laptop where the store is blocked, on a Chromebook, on an iPad, and in a private window.

The two real limits of any web route: a single video at a time, and the fact that a third-party page learns which URL you asked about. If either matters to you, Route 2 or Route 3 below. For the four-step version of the workflow, see our guide to downloading captions from a YouTube video for free.

Route 2: the transcript panel and Ctrl+C

The zero-dependency method. Under the description, Show transcript opens the caption text in a side panel; the panel’s own menu lets you hide the timestamps, which you want before copying. Then select the text, copy, paste into a document, and you have a transcript with no tool, no site and no sign-in involved — the most private option of the three, because the only party involved is YouTube itself.

Why it isn’t the default: it produces text, not a caption file. You can’t get an SRT out of it, so it’s useless for editing; and it degrades badly with length. On a 45-minute interview, copying the panel gives you rolled auto-caption segments repeated where they overlap, sentences split across lines and one speaker merged into another. Our walkthrough of how to copy all captions from a YouTube video documents the whole manual process and the exact clean-up it needs, so read that before you spend twenty minutes selecting text.

Route 3: yt-dlp — nothing in the browser, everything on disk

If your objection to extensions is “I don’t want a third party seeing my links”, the answer isn’t a web tool, it’s a local program. yt-dlp is the open-source archiver maintained on GitHub; these flags pull captions and only captions:

yt-dlp --skip-download --write-subs --write-auto-subs \
       --sub-langs "en,es,ja" --convert-subs srt "VIDEO_URL"

Your browser keeps its permissions clean, and this is the only route that does a playlist: point it at a playlist URL and you get a folder of .srt files. The trade-off is the setup — Python plus a terminal, the odd update when YouTube changes something, and no point-and-click interface. Desktop “subtitle downloader” apps are the same idea with a GUI, but they are a real install, usually a licence after a trial, and worth avoiding for a task you do twice a month.

The middle ground: bookmarklets and userscripts

For people who genuinely want a button on the YouTube page without granting extension permissions, two things get close:

  • A bookmarklet — a snippet of JavaScript saved as a bookmark, run by clicking it on the video page. Nothing installed, nothing persistent, gone when you delete the bookmark. The catch is you have to trust code you pasted yourself, and it can’t touch the page’s network requests the way an extension can.
  • A userscript in a manager such as Tampermonkey — more capable and closer to an extension in practice, but the manager itself is an extension with broad access, so you’ve traded one permission problem for another.

We’d only bother with either if you download captions several times a day and the paste-a-URL step is what’s slowing you down.

Frequently asked questions

Can I download YouTube captions without installing an extension?

Yes. A browser caption downloader needs nothing installed: paste the video URL, choose a track and download SRT, VTT or TXT. YouTube’s own transcript panel gives you text by copy-paste, and yt-dlp runs locally from a terminal. None of the three requires an extension.

Why should I avoid caption-download extensions?

To work, an extension needs permission to read and change data on the pages it touches, and many request all sites rather than just YouTube. Extensions can also be sold or updated later without the permission list changing. Saving four steps in a browser tool is not worth persistent access to your browsing.

Is a website safer than an extension for downloading captions?

Different risk, not zero risk. A web tool sees the URL you paste and nothing else about your session, then the tab closes and the access ends. An extension keeps access the whole time it is installed. Choose a tool that states plainly what it does — ours says no signup and no install on the homepage — and avoid any page that wants a Google sign-in for a public caption file.

What is the closest thing to an extension without installing one?

A bookmarklet or a userscript run from a manager such as Tampermonkey gives you a button on the YouTube page without a browser extension. Both are worth doing only if you download captions many times a day; for occasional use a web tool is faster to start and easier to walk away from.
