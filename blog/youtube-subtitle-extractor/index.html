TXT vs SRT vs VTT: which subtitle format to download?

The extractor’s three output formats are not interchangeable, and picking the right one saves a conversion step later:

  • TXT — plain text with the timecodes stripped. This is the transcript format: paste it into a doc, turn a webinar into a blog post, drop a lecture into your notes, or hand it to an AI assistant to summarize. If your goal is reading or repurposing the words, download YouTube subtitles as TXT.
  • SRT — the decades-old SubRip format that became the universal subtitle standard: numbered cues with start and end timecodes. Premiere Pro, Final Cut, DaVinci Resolve, VLC and most editors and media players expect it, so SRT is the safe default when the file’s destination is a video.
  • VTT — WebVTT, the W3C-standard caption format (full spec here) that the HTML5 <track> element and modern web players use. Same cue structure as SRT with richer styling hooks; pick it when the subtitles are headed for a website rather than an editor.

All three download from the same table, so the practical answer is: match the format to the destination and re-extract if you guessed wrong — it costs you ten seconds, not a re-render.

Official captions and 150+ auto-translated languages

When you extract subtitles from a YouTube video, the results table splits into two tiers. First come the official tracks — captions the uploader wrote or uploaded themselves, in whatever languages they published. Then, when the video has auto-captions, the tool also lists more than 150 auto-translated languages alongside the official tracks, generated from YouTube’s automatic translation. That second tier is what makes the tool unusually useful for cross-language work: you can read a Japanese lecture in English or an English podcast in Spanish with one click.

Set expectations accordingly. Auto-captions are only as accurate as the audio they were generated from — heavy accents, overlapping speakers and jargon degrade them — and auto-translations are machine-grade. For research, comprehension and draft work they’re excellent; for accessibility or anything you publish under your own name, prefer an official track where one exists. YouTube’s own help documentation explains how its captions, auto-captions and translated tracks work, and it’s worth a skim if you’ll rely on the extracted text.

Six things to do with an extracted subtitle file

Subtitle files are one of the most versatile assets in a content workflow, which is why “extract subtitles from YouTube” searches span so many different jobs. Six we keep coming back to:

  • Turn videos into blog posts. A transcript is the fastest first draft there is — paste the TXT export into an editor, tighten it, add structure. Repurposing video this way is also a legitimate SEO play: search engines can finally read what the video said.
  • Caption your own edits. Pull SRT files and burn them in with a browser editor — our VideoEditorOnline review covers a free one that adds subtitles without uploading your footage. For more options, browse our free video tools category.
  • Accessibility. SRT or VTT captions make a video usable for deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers — and for the majority of social viewers watching with the sound off.
  • Language learning. Auto-translated tracks make crude but effective dual subtitles: the original language on the video, your language in the file.
  • Research and quoting. Cite exact wording from a talk or interview without scrubbing the timeline, and search a long video like a document.
  • Competitive keyword research. Extract the transcript of a competitor’s most-viewed video and you can see exactly which phrases and hooks they repeat — audience research on a platter.

Where it falls short

No tool write-up is complete without the fine print. Three limits are worth knowing before you bookmark it:

  • One video at a time. There’s no batch mode and no playlist handling — you paste one URL, extract, repeat. Fine for most people; a chore if you regularly process dozens of videos.
  • It extracts, it doesn’t create. The tool pulls existing caption tracks; it doesn’t transcribe audio itself. A video with no captions and no auto-captions sends you away empty-handed.
  • No editing built in. Timing off, or a typo inherited from the source? You’ll fix that in a text editor or an SRT-aware tool after download — the extractor hands you the file and steps aside.

None of these are surprises — they’re the boundary of the tool’s one job — but they define who should look elsewhere: anyone needing live transcription, batch processing or subtitle editing should budget for a dedicated tool instead.
