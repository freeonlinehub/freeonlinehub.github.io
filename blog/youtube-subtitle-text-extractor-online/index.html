Why raw subtitle text is a mess

Every frustration here traces back to the fact that captions were authored to be *read on screen for two seconds*, not read as a document:

  • Duplicated runs. Automatic captioning re-sends the tail of a segment while new words arrive, so the same clause appears two or three times in a row. This is the number one complaint and it’s structural, not a bug.
  • Line breaks in mid-sentence. Cue boundaries become newlines, so paragraphs shatter into 40-character fragments.
  • No punctuation on auto tracks. Sentences run together, which makes the text hard to skim even after dedupe.
  • No speaker labels. Interviews read as one person’s monologue.
  • Music and filler cues. “♪ ♪”, “[applause]”, and every “um” the microphone caught.
  • Short-segment spam. A single sentence spread across five cues, one line per word group.

None of it means the extractor failed. It means the track you pulled is a caption track, and captions need a conversion pass before they become text you’d show anyone. The W3C’s captioning guidance explains the on-screen constraints that produce this shape.

The six-minute clean-up pass

Do it in order in any editor with find-and-replace (VS Code, Notepad++, BBEdit, Sublime — all handle regex; plain Notepad doesn’t).

  1. Collapse duplicate consecutive lines. Search backreference for a repeated line, replace with one copy — run twice, it catches the triple repeats:
# regex find → replace
^(.+)\n\1$      →   $1
  1. Strip leftover cue debris if you converted an SRT rather than downloading TXT — numbering lines and timecode lines:
^\d+$                                        (delete)
^\d{2}:\d{2}:\d{2}[,.]\d{3}.*-->.*$          (delete)
  1. Join the shattered lines. Replace single newlines inside a paragraph with spaces, then restore breaks at real paragraph ends. In most editors: select all, join to one line, then re-break every ~90 characters or at sentence ends.
  2. Remove music/noise cues: search ♪, [music], [applause] and delete.
  3. Fix the names. Scan for proper nouns the auto track mangled — product names, people, cities. This is the step that decides whether the text is trustworthy.
  4. Break speakers apart by hand. On an interview, put a paragraph break wherever the voice changes and label it. Twenty minutes on a 45-minute conversation; do it once and you’ll never paste an unlabeled auto transcript into an article again.

Punctuation on auto tracks is the one thing you can’t regex away — it needs reading. That’s also why we’d never ship an unedited auto transcript as a blog post: YouTube’s caption docs describe automatic tracks as the lowest tier of the three, and it shows.

Formatting for where the text is going

  • Article draft. Keep the TXT as an appendix file, write from it rather than in it, so the source stays intact for quotes.
  • Notes and study. Add a heading every few minutes using what the cue at that point said — the document becomes navigable without timestamps.
  • AI prompts. Clean text first: duplicated lines and cue debris burn tokens and quietly degrade the summary. For that step you may want timestamps preserved instead, which is the SRT case.
  • Accessibility. Use the manual track or fix the text properly; a wall of unpunctuated auto-caption text helps nobody.
  • Republishing someone else’s words. Get permission or paraphrase — see our note on fair use and the creator’s rights framing in our caption extraction guide.

Minutes to words: how much text you should expect

Useful for judging whether an extractor actually gave you everything, and for planning an article. Conversational English runs roughly 140–160 words a minute, so a 10-minute video yields about 1,400–1,600 words before clean-up and maybe 1,100–1,300 after dedupe. If your “10-minute transcript” is 400 words, you pulled a partial or heavily translated track; if it’s 4,000, the duplicates didn’t get stripped. Auto tracks on non-English audio also produce notably shorter files, because translation cues drop more text than the source language keeps.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get YouTube subtitles as plain text?

Download the TXT version of the caption track. In youtube-caption-downloader.com that means pasting the video URL, picking the track and choosing TXT instead of SRT or VTT: you get the words with timecodes and cue numbers removed, ready to paste into a document.

Why does my extracted subtitle text repeat the same lines?

Because automatic captioning resends the tail of each segment so the on-screen captions stay readable while words carry over. The result is duplicated phrases every few lines. Removing consecutive duplicate lines fixes most of it in one pass — the regex is in the clean-up section above.

Is TXT better than SRT for reading a transcript?

Yes, if you are going to read or reuse the words. TXT has no timing data to get in the way and pastes cleanly into any editor. Keep SRT when the file’s destination is a video editor or player, because there you need the timecodes.

Can a text extractor handle accented or non-Latin subtitles?

Yes, as long as you save and open the file as UTF-8. Accent marks, Cyrillic, Chinese and Arabic come through intact; mojibake means something re-saved the file in a legacy encoding, not that the extractor broke. This guide covers the encoding fixes.
